One of my students asked me the other day about the odds for non-PDI-P parties in Bali's legislative elections. I hadn't given the question much thought until then. Bali has long been a bastion of the Banteng, as PDI-P is known after its bull emblem, alongside Central Java, though the party's standing slipped slightly in the 2024 legislative election. So I played around with some data at my disposal and came up with a few visualizations. The result is below.

## Where PDI-P's rivals thrive

**In 2019, PDI-P won 54% of Bali's legislative vote and 51 of 57 kecamatan.** Its rivals' strength is concentrated in four kinds of place:

| Zone | Where | Main beneficiaries |
|---|---|---|
| **The dry east** | Karangasem, Nusa Penida (Klungkung), Kintamani uplands, north-east Buleleng | Golkar |
| **The Muslim coast** | Jembrana and Buleleng coasts, the old *kampung*, parts of Denpasar | PKB, PKS, PAN |
| **Personal bastions** | Single villages, e.g. Awan, Bugbug, Dukuh, Peliatan | Whichever party fields the local figure |
| **The urban south** | Southern Badung, Denpasar | Many parties; no single winner |

![PDI-P's rivals win in the east, not the south](/images/blog/bali-competitive-fringe/fig0-rival-tps.png)

**At the level of individual polling stations, the contrast is starker.** In 2019, PDI-P's rivals came first at more than half of Karangasem's polling stations (57%) and at two in five in Klungkung. In Tabanan, Denpasar and Badung, they won fewer than one in ten. Even within the Banteng's island fortress, the east is genuinely contested ground.

![The competitive districts have been the same for twenty years](/images/blog/bali-competitive-fringe/fig1-history.png)

**This geography is old.** On the district-council ballot the same districts have led for two decades. Klungkung and Karangasem were the most competitive in 2004 and remained so in 2014; that year Gerindra won Klungkung's council vote outright. Tabanan was the least competitive throughout.

**Competition has grown everywhere at local level,** even in Tabanan (from 25% to 44% non-PDI-P). Local races give minor parties and local figures far more room than national ones.

**Golkar's hold on the east is more recent.** In 2004 its strongest districts were Gianyar and Badung. By 2014 Karangasem had become its stronghold.

**The two maps below line up.** The light-blue core of PDI-P's strength in the top map is Tabanan, Badung and the south-centre, the same belt that is green, wet-rice country, in the bottom map. The dark-blue zones where PDI-P's rivals do best are Karangasem, Nusa Penida, the north and the west, which are mostly orange, dry-crop farming. Bali's Muslim-majority villages (white dots) sit on the coasts, west, north and east, on Nusa Penida and in Denpasar.

![Maps of Bali: the non-PDI-P share of the 2019 village vote, and the main economy of each village](/images/blog/bali-competitive-fringe/map-vote-economy.jpg)

So, to my student's question: the odds are real, but they are local. PDI-P's rivals thrive in the same few places they always have, and the map has barely moved in twenty years.
