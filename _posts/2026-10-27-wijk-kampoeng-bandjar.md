*–wijk, kampoeng, bandjar.*

<p class="post-tags"><span class="tag">#history</span> <span class="tag">#Bali</span></p>

Singaraja is my hometown. I grew up in Kampung Tinggi, east of the river near the old harbour, and I thought I knew the town's layout well enough. Then I came across a hand-drawn map of it, folded into a Dutch journal article from 1939. The article is C. J. Grader's study of a single temple, the Pura Pamajoen of Banjar Tegal. The map is there to show where Banjar Tegal sits. It ends up showing a good deal more than that.

The title block reads *Schets desa Boeleleng weergevende de woonwijken en bandjar's*: a sketch of the village of Buleleng, showing the residential quarters and *banjar*s. Note the word "village". In 1939 Singaraja was the Dutch seat of government for Bali, with a harbour, a European quarter and trading communities from across the archipelago. Grader still calls it a *désa*. The red numbers are mine.

First, how to hold it. There is no north arrow, but the harbour (10) is at the top, so north is roughly up and the hills are at the bottom. For a Balinese reader this flips the usual sense of direction. In Buleleng, *kaja*, towards the mountain, is south, and *kelod*, towards the sea, is north. Grader uses exactly these terms when he divides Banjar Tegal into four quarters.

Now the people. The map does not label anyone by ethnicity directly. It does it with nouns. Around the harbour are three *wijk*s: Chinese (1), Arab (5) and Buginese (7). *Wijk* was the Dutch administrative word for an ethnic quarter under its own headman. Nearby are the *kampoeng*s: Tinggi (6), my own, Kadjanan (8) and Bali (9), with a Muslim burial ground (3) among them. Further inland are the *bandjar*s: Djawa, Tengah, Pegoejangan, Tegal, Paketan, Liligoendi and others. In Grader's usage the *banjar* is the Balinese ward that belongs to the village's temple community. Three words, three kinds of belonging.

Which makes Kampoeng Bali (9) the odd one out. A settlement called "Bali" is classed as a *kampoeng*, not a *banjar*. My guess is that its people were Balinese who had come from elsewhere, and so stood outside the local temple community. When I was a kid, I had friends there whose families came from Karangasem, from Bugbug and the villages around it. But I don't know for sure.

The map sorts us. We did not stay sorted. Kampung Tinggi sits right next to the Chinese *wijk*, so when I say *lu* and *gua*, I am not pretending to be from Jakarta. It is how I talked with my Chinese friends as a kid. I also say *ana*, as the Arabs do, because we had friends in Kampung Kajanan, most of them Arab Muslims. And so on, down the list of quarters. People in Singaraja have always been cosmopolitan in this small, everyday way. The Dutch drew the lines, and the children talked across them.

Then the spine. The hatched strip (28) is the European residential quarter. It runs straight down the main road from the harbour to the inland end of town, splitting the place lengthwise. Offices, officials' houses and the road to the interior all sit on it. The Balinese *banjar*s line up on either side, Tegal and Banyumala to the west, Jawa, Tengah and Peguyangan to the east.

What the map leaves out is just as telling. Of all the temples in a Balinese town, it marks only three: Pura Segara (2) by the harbour, Pura Dalem (17) beside the burial ground, and Pura Desa (24) at the southern crossroads. These are the three village temples that every *banjar* worships. They run along the sea–mountain axis, from the sea temple at the shore to the village temple inland. Grader's argument is that a *désa* is a worship community that binds "a large number of component units of great variety" into one. His map makes the same argument. It is less a town plan than a picture of that community, with the colonial town laid over it.

Do the names survive? Many do. Today's Kecamatan Buleleng has [kelurahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_districts_and_sub-districts_in_Buleleng_Regency){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} called Banjar Jawa, Banjar Tegal, Kampung Kajanan, Kampung Bugis, Liligundi, Paket Agung and Beratan. There is also a Banjar Bali. Whether that is the old Kampoeng Bali promoted, I cannot tell from a sketch at 1:20,000. Boundaries have moved, and the three old nouns have since been flattened into one administrative word.

So the map answers a question I had not thought to ask: how did the colonial town sort the people in it? Mostly by vocabulary. And what held the town together, in Grader's eyes, was not the road down the middle but the three temples along it.
