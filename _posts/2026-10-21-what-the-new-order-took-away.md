*–on two essays, and the legacy of an absence*

<p class="post-tags"><span class="tag">#democracy</span> <span class="tag">#history</span></p>

This year I wrote two essays for the CEU Democracy Institute's *Review of Democracy*. Read separately, they look like two different projects. They are really one argument, told from opposite ends.

The first, written with Iqra Anugrah and published in March, is [a reassessment of the New Order's authoritarian legacies](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/03/03/indonesias-democratic-decline/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. Indonesia has held competitive elections since 1999, yet over the past decade its democracy has thinned: courts that bend, a shrinking civic space, oligarchs with ever more say, and soldiers back in civilian jobs. Much of the commentary explains this through the people in charge, their coalitions and their style, and so tends to start the clock in 2014. We argued for a longer clock. The New Order left behind institutions, networks of elites and capital, and habits of thought, and these still shape what democratic politicians can and cannot do. Some of that inheritance is an *endowment*, which democrats can put to new use. Some of it is a *deficit*, which drags on any attempt to deepen democracy.

The second essay, published this week, is [*The Twilight of the Modernizers*](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/10/20/twilight-modernizers/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. It looks at a group that the legacy story tends to leave out. The New Order was not built by generals and cronies alone. In 1965–66 it also had technocrats and student activists on its side, people who wanted stabilisation, planning, and cleaner government. Within a decade both groups had been pushed aside. The economists were overruled when it mattered most, as in Pertamina's borrowing spree, and the students were repressed once their protests turned to corruption and to Soeharto himself. The oil boom made both groups dispensable: a regime flush with petrodollars no longer needed sober economic advice, and it no longer needed to tolerate its young critics. Neither group had a party or a mass base to fall back on. By the 1990s many of them were in opposition.

Put the two essays side by side and the connection is plain. The usual account of authoritarian legacies is a story about *presence*: who and what survived 1998. The modernizers' story is about *absence*: what had already been removed by 1978. The two are the same fact seen from different angles. Predatory elites carried over smoothly into democracy partly because the people best placed to check them inside the old regime had been purged a generation earlier. When the transition came, Indonesia inherited the New Order's machinery and personnel, but not a reformist wing with its own institutions, its own followers, and the habit of saying no from within.

In the vocabulary of the March essay, that is a deficit, and an unusual one, because it does not show up anywhere. You cannot point to a modernist faction that obstructs reform, since there is none. What you can see are the consequences: a revolving door between the old order and the new, and a democratic elite that reaches for New Order tools whenever they are useful.

The lesson is not nostalgic. The technocrats and students of the 1960s were no democrats in waiting, and many of them served the regime willingly for years. The point is about the structure of the problem. Democracies are rarely undone only by the people who oppose them. They are also undone by the slow loss of the people inside who could slow things down. Indonesia lost its insiders early, which may be why backsliding has met so little resistance from within the state.

The other three essays in the series fill in the rest of the picture. Norman Joshua [traces the legal side](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/03/11/when-soldiers-became-jurists/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}: in the 1950s, army officers and civilian jurists turned martial law into lasting institutions, so the legal groundwork for military rule was laid before the New Order existed. Iqra Anugrah [looks at the ideas](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/04/07/talking-democracy-while-tearing-it/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, showing how Ali Moertopo and his circle of conservatives could dismantle democracy while claiming to save it. Farabi Fakih [places the regime within the American financial empire](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/06/03/arena-battle-capitals-managers/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, where rival camps of technocrats and technologists, each with its own source of foreign currency, competed to run the economy. His account helps explain my modernizers' fall: once oil money gave the other camp its own source of funds, the economists' advice mattered less. I recommend all three, though I am hardly a neutral party.
