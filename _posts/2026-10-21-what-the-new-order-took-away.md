*–on two essays, and the legacy of an absence*

<p class="post-tags"><span class="tag">#democracy</span> <span class="tag">#history</span></p>


This year I wrote two essays for the CEU Democracy Institute's *Review of Democracy*. These two essays are outputs of several workshops I organized with my colleague Iqra Anugrah.

The first, written with Iqra Anugrah, came out in March. It is [a reassessment of the New Order's authoritarian legacies](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/03/03/indonesias-democratic-decline/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. Indonesia has held competitive elections since 1999, yet over the past decade its democracy has thinned. Courts bend. Civic space shrinks. Oligarchs have more say, and soldiers are back in civilian jobs. Most commentary blames the people in charge, their coalitions and their style, and so starts the clock in 2014. We argued for a longer clock. The New Order left behind institutions, networks of elites and capital, and habits of thought that still shape what democratic politicians can do. Some of that inheritance is an *endowment*, which democrats can put to new use; some of it is a *deficit*, which drags on reform.

The second, out this week, is [*The Twilight of the Modernizers*](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/10/20/twilight-modernizers/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. It is about a group the legacy story tends to leave out. Generals and cronies did not build the New Order alone: in 1965–66, technocrats and student activists stood with it too, hoping for stabilisation, planning, and cleaner government. Within a decade both had been pushed aside. The economists were overruled when it mattered most, as in Pertamina's borrowing spree, and the students were repressed once they turned on corruption, and on Soeharto himself. The oil boom made both dispensable. A regime flush with petrodollars needed neither sober advice nor young critics. Economists everywhere will know the feeling. Political scientists, at least, are rarely asked in the first place. With no party or mass base to fall back on, many of the modernizers had drifted into opposition by the 1990s.

Put the two side by side and the link is plain. The usual legacy story is about *presence*: who and what survived 1998. The modernizers' story is about *absence*: what was already gone by 1978. They are the same fact, seen from two angles. Predatory elites carried over into democracy with ease, partly because the insiders best placed to check them had been purged a generation earlier. Indonesia inherited the New Order's machinery and its people, but not a reformist wing with its own institutions, its own followers, and the habit of saying no from within.

In the March essay's terms, that is a deficit, and an odd one, because it leaves no trace. You cannot point to a modernist faction blocking reform. There is none. What you can see are the effects: a revolving door between the old order and the new, and a democratic elite that reaches for New Order tools whenever they come in handy.

This is not nostalgia. The technocrats and students of the 1960s were no democrats in waiting, and many served the regime willingly for years. The point is structural. Democracies are undone not only by their enemies but by the slow loss of insiders who could apply the brakes. Indonesia lost its insiders early, which may be why backsliding has met so little resistance from within the state.

The other three essays fill in the picture. Norman Joshua [traces the legal side](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/03/11/when-soldiers-became-jurists/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}: in the 1950s, army officers and civilian jurists turned martial law into lasting institutions, laying the groundwork for military rule before the New Order existed. Iqra Anugrah [looks at the ideas](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/04/07/talking-democracy-while-tearing-it/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, showing how Ali Moertopo and his circle could dismantle democracy while claiming to save it. Farabi Fakih [places the regime within the American financial empire](https://revdem.ceu.edu/2026/06/03/arena-battle-capitals-managers/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, where rival camps of technocrats and technologists, each with its own source of foreign currency, fought to run the economy. His account helps explain my modernizers' fall: once oil money gave the other camp its own funds, the economists' advice mattered less. I recommend all three, though I am hardly a neutral party.
