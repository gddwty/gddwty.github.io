*–not so common, apparently.*

To understand an Indonesian politician, we have long looked to their formative years: where they were born, where they grew up, how they came of age as a politician. The biographical approach has a long pedigree, from accounts of General Soeharto ([here](https://opac.perpusnas.go.id/DetailOpac.aspx?id=98608){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}) to, more recently, of President Joko Widodo ([here](https://www.amazon.com/Man-Contradictions-Widodo-struggle-Indonesia/dp/1760897248){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}).

A familiar trope of the genre is the politician's humble origins, which are said to explain a lifelong quest to lift up the marginalized, economically, socially and politically. "I am one of the *rakyat*, the common people": that, more or less, is the message. Whether it is sincere or merely performance is for you to judge, should you ever have the privilege of knowing the politician in person.

I, for one, was curious to see where Jokowi used to spend his time in Solo, and above all where he liked to eat. According to the internet, one of his favorite places in his Solo days was [Soto Triwindu](https://travel.kompas.com/read/2019/08/26/200000427/menikmati-soto-triwindu-yang-jadi-favorit-jokowi-di-kota-solo?page=all){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}, a beef or chicken soup named after the traditional market where the stall stands.

I count myself a big fan of soto: not yet a connoisseur, but always ready for a bowl, anytime, anywhere. I can confirm that Soto Triwindu is delicious, among the very best of its kind. But here is the important part: it is also, at least on first impression, a rather fancy soto place.

If soto is the food of commoners, Soto Triwindu is soto royalty. It is certainly not the kind of *kaki lima* street stall I visit every morning. What sets it apart is not the price; it remains well within reach of Jakarta's middle class. It is the presentation. Triwindu feels more like a restaurant than a street stall. The fried tempeh, the beef tripe (*babat*) and the other side dishes are kept in the glass drawers of a rather handsome piece of furniture, to name just one of its touches.

Now suppose this really is Jokowi's favorite soto, and that it says something about the kind of person he is. Is he a commoner like us? Yes and no. He is indeed a commoner, all the more so when seen from Jakarta, where the middle class frequents fancy ethnic restaurants and Western food franchises. But among commoners, Jokowi belongs to the upper tier. He is cultured, he has taste, and, more importantly, he can afford it. People tend to forget that by the time he began his electoral career, he was already a self-made man, if on a provincial scale: a successful furniture businessman exporting his products around the world.

Small wonder, then, that the soto he favors arrives with its tempeh and tripe displayed in fine furniture.
